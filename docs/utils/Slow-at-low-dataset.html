Cold Start at Low N: Why BPlusTreeMap Loses Before It Wins

This is a study of one specific regime: BPlusTreeMap running cold — starting from an empty tree, no warm-up — at low key counts. It's not one benchmark and it's not one bug. It started with a single redundant binary search inside merge(), but chasing that down small-N cold behavior kept turning up more: a benchmark-annotation bug that was quietly inflating every per-key API's reported cost at 100k, an eager-allocation design choice that costs a small, flat tax on every empty node, and a crossover with java.util.TreeMap that only shows up once size and warm-up state are separated out instead of conflated. Every wrong turn below is left in — the value here is the sequence of theories that profiling and cross-checking killed, not just the final numbers.

Round 1: The Merge Benchmark Loses

It started, like most of these do, with a benchmark that hurt my expectation. I ran a merge benchmark against TreeMap, fully expecting to win. I didn't. Before even touching performance, I found an actual bug sitting in the middle of the merge path — modCount was being tracked inconsistently across the operation, so I refactored that first. Correctness before speed.

Round 2: The Two-Traversal Tax

With the bug fixed, I went looking for the slowdown itself. Turned out remove() was doing the classic rookie move: find the key in one traversal, then remove it in a second, separate traversal. Two trips down the tree for one logical operation. I collapsed that into a single pass.

Ran the benchmark again. Still ~20ns slower than TreeMap. Somewhere, I was still paying for something I didn't need to pay for.

Round 3: Finding the Real Waste

So I stopped micro-tuning and actually traced the shape of the operation top to bottom. Going down the tree, I do a binary search at every level to route to the right child — that part's unavoidable, it's the whole point of a B+Tree. But on the way back up, when a split or merge needs to know a node's position in its parent, I was doing another binary search to re-derive information I basically already had on the way down. Same work, done twice, for no real reason. That redundancy was the real target.

The Three Traps

I went through four ideas to kill that redundant search. The first three all turned out to be traps.

1. The Depth Stack

Idea: keep a fixed-size (32-deep) int[] stack on the way down that remembers position at each depth, then pop it on the way back up instead of re-searching.

The Trap: The whole plan rests on the JVM's Escape Analysis stack-allocating that array so it's effectively free. But EA is not a promise — it's a best-effort optimization that can silently bail out under the wrong conditions. I'd be betting real performance on a compiler heuristic I don't control and can't fully predict.

2. The ThreadLocal Buffer

Idea: sidestep allocation entirely with a ThreadLocal<int[]> depth buffer, reused per thread.

The Trap: This only holds up if the caller's thread stays "clean." The moment someone wraps a call in a synchronized block or a reentrant lock in a way that re-enters the tree on the same thread, the buffer state gets corrupted out from under you. Rejected.

3. The Node-Level Healer Field

Idea: add a small tracking field directly to the node — call it a "healer" slot — that gets populated during parsing and just tells the node its own index in the parent, no search needed.

The Trap: It works, but it's a standing memory cost on every node, all the time, to save a search that only matters occasionally. Small per node, but it adds up across the whole tree for a benefit that doesn't scale with it.

The Fourth Trap I Almost Missed: Turning a Clean Read into a Dirty Write

There was one more angle I nearly skipped. What if I just updated the traceHealer field only during put() descents, leaving reads alone? That sounds harmless, but it introduces a real hardware penalty: I'd be dirtying cache lines that never needed to be touched. Right now, the descent phase of a mutation is entirely read-only until it hits the leaf — the CPU streams the arrays through L1 at full speed, no side effects. If I force the tree to save its path by writing curr.childIdx = idx on every hop, I turn a pristine, read-only descent into a series of memory writes. Every write marks that cache line dirty, and a dirty line eventually has to be written back — down to L2, possibly further — whether or not I ever use traceHealer for that particular put. Reads never pay this: they stream through, leave the line clean, and it can just be evicted for free when the cache needs the space. The "useless" binary search on the bubble-up phase is actually cheaper than forcing the CPU to write to memory on the way down.

Confirming It With JFR

Before committing to "just eat the search," I wanted data, not intuition. I profiled the merge benchmark with JFR. The hot-methods view told the real story: inside BPlusTreeMap, roughly 65% of samples during merge landed in Arrays.binarySearch0, with the tree's own merge() method itself accounting for about 12–13% of samples. Allocation pressure was dominated by the map's own entry objects and backing arrays, not by anything exotic.

The Realization

After rejecting all three, I went back and actually measured how often that upward propagation happens. A split or merge that propagates up only triggers around the 2t boundary of a node — and as the degree increases, that boundary gets hit less and less often relative to total operations.

I was trying to eliminate a cost that barely happens, by paying a cost on every operation that does.

That's the amortization quirk: the honest fix isn't a cleverer data structure to avoid the search — it's recognizing that the search is already rare, and letting it stay rare instead of engineering around it. Just like ArrayList eats an array-copy tax during resizing to keep add() at O(1) the rest of the time, the tree eats one binary search at propagation time to keep every other operation clean.

The fix wasn't a smarter structure. It was noticing the cost I was trying to avoid almost never shows up.

Part 2: The Extended Investigation — Cold Start Across Size

A performance autopsy on ChaosTree's cold-start behavior, picking up where the merge() investigation left off. Chasing cache locality, allocation pressure, and binary search overhead across four orders of magnitude turned up two more real bugs and two design tradeoffs — including one benchmark bug (below) that was silently inflating every 100k number in an earlier draft of this post by ~10x.

The setup

ChaosTree is a zero-dependency Java tree library, and one of its map implementations — BPlusTreeMap — was benchmarked head-to-head against java.util.TreeMap across four orders of magnitude: 1,000 / 10,000 / 100,000 / 1,000,000 keys. The results weren't a clean win or loss — they were a crossover:

Benchmark setup

All numbers in this post come from NaryTreeMapUpdateBenchmark, a JMH avgt (average time) benchmark comparing BPlusTreeMap against java.util.TreeMap across their shared API surface (put, putIfAbsent, merge, compute, computeIfAbsent, computeIfPresent, plus a baseline insert-only case).

Two JMH params matter for reading every table below:

  • comparator=true — a Collections.reverseOrder()-style wrapping comparator is used instead of natural ordering, adding one extra virtual dispatch per comparison for both implementations.
  • preFill — this is the one that actually explains most of the swings in this post. preFill=false means each benchmark iteration starts from an empty tree (cold: no warm-up, near-empty structure). preFill=true means the tree is pre-loaded to size entries before the timed portion starts (warm: JIT and caches are hot, structure is at steady-state depth).

The crossover numbers quoted throughout ("BPlusTreeMap loses at 1k/10k, wins at 100k/1M") come from the preFill=false (cold) rows specifically. As the raw evidence below shows, the cold-start picture is not flat across sizes — the gap narrows substantially as the dataset grows and the result crosses over between 10k and 100k.

My first instinct, looking at the small-N losses, was architectural: a red-black tree is nodes-and-pointers, no array maintenance. My B+Tree stores keys in per-node arrays and has to do array bookkeeping — shifting, splitting, propagating — on every insert. Surely that was the cost.

I was wrong about almost everything for the first half of this investigation, and the data proved it every time. That's the actual value of this post: not the final fix, but the sequence of plausible-sounding theories that profiling killed one by one — and the fact that the small-N loss and the large-N win turned out to be caused by two unrelated mechanisms, not one story that flips sign.

Two more findings from the cold-path audit

Why baseline stays completely flat across size

baseline cold doesn't move at all between 10,000 and 100,000 — 6.5–7.1ns/op for BPlusTreeMap, 4.3–4.8ns/op for JavaTreeMap, regardless of size. That's expected, not a bug: the method is just 

public Map<Integer, Integer> baseline() {
    return transformer.apply(supplier.get());
}

— one constructor call, no loop over keys. size only controls how many keys get shuffled into the keys array during @Setup; baseline never touches that array, so there's no path by which it could scale with size.

The ~2ns gap itself comes from a real, small design choice: every BPlusTreeMap node — including a brand-new, empty root — eagerly allocates full 2 × degree capacity at construction. At degree=64 that's a 128-length key array and a 129-length child array, plus a keyCount and a parent pointer, all allocated before a single key is ever inserted. TreeMap's constructor, by contrast, is close to a single field assignment — it defers all node allocation to the first put. Roughly 1–2KB of upfront array payload per empty node is a small cost for one root, but it's paid by every node in the tree, not just the one baseline happens to measure — worth revisiting if node count ever gets memory-sensitive, independent of the ns/op story here.

Why cold computeIfPresent is near-zero for both — but still 8x apart

Cold computeIfPresent sits at 0.15–0.16ns/op (BPlusTreeMap) vs. 0.017–0.02ns/op (JavaTreeMap) at both 10k and 100k — flat across size, for both implementations, unlike every other API in these tables. That flatness is the tell: on an empty, cold tree, computeIfPresent(key, ...) never finds the key, so the remapping function never runs. Both implementations are measuring "look up in an empty structure, find nothing, return null" — genuinely near-zero work, not a real search — so it's expected to stay flat rather than scale with size.

Part 1: Why BPlusTreeMap loses at small N (1,000–10,000 keys)

Theory 1: "Array maintenance is inherently slower than pointer surgery"

The initial hypothesis: a red-black tree's rebalancing is O(1) local pointer fixup, while a B-tree's bottom-up propagation requires "massive array calculation" at every level.

First JFR profile (comparator=true, BPlusTreeMap, size=10000, cold): 

Arrays.binarySearch0                    44.89%
Collections$ReverseComparator.compare   21.02%
Integer.compare                         14.20%
BPlusTreeMap.merge                       9.09%
AbstractNaryMapNode.<init>               5.68%

No array-copy or split method anywhere near the top. The dominant cost was search, not propagation. Theory 1 was dead on arrival — the actual hot path was Arrays.binarySearch0 calling into a comparator, not array shifting during rebalance.

Theory 2: "It's the JDK binary search method's overhead"

Next theory: maybe Arrays.binarySearch0's generic bounds-checking and dispatch machinery was the tax, and a hand-rolled binary search loop, inlined directly into the search method, would remove it.

I'd already found per-key-type linear-search thresholds (32 for Integer, 8 for String, 12 generic) by empirically finding the linear/binary crossover point for each comparator cost profile. I wired those in, replaced Arrays.binarySearch with a manual loop, and reran.

Result: 147.591 ns/op → 144.808 ns/op. A 2% improvement. Nothing.

The profile explained why: 

searchNodeMap (manual loop)              45.18%   [was binarySearch0 at 44.89%]
ReverseComparator.compare                19.88%   [was 21.02%]

The exact same percentage, just relabeled under a different method name. Arrays.binarySearch0 was never scaffolding — it was already a tight loop. I'd rewritten the same cost by hand and congratulated myself on nothing.

This also surfaced a fanout problem separate from the search-algorithm question entirely: the tree's degree was 64, meaning every node held 63–127 keys — always above even the largest linear-search threshold (32). The threshold logic I'd built was correct and completely inert, because it could never trigger at the fanout actually being benchmarked.

Theory 3: "It's excessive allocation — my nodes allocate too much"

allocation-by-site showed 92.37% of allocation pressure coming from node construction (AbstractNaryMapNode.<init>), versus Java's TreeMap.addEntry at 62.54%. Looked damning.

It wasn't. Comparing absolute bytes, not relative percentages:

  • Java: 1.2 GB total allocated
  • BPlusTreeMap: 339.6 MB total allocated

Java allocated 3.5x more garbage over the same run and was still faster. 92% of a small number is not automatically bigger than 62% of a large one — relative allocation-site percentages from two different processes are not comparable without the absolute totals. This was the most instructive dead end of the whole investigation: a metric that looked alarming in isolation and meant the opposite once normalized correctly.

Theory 4: "It's L3 cache locality — my scattered array nodes thrash the cache while RBT pointer-chasing stays local"

This felt like the strongest theory yet at the time, and it matched the widening relative gap between 10k and 1k (22% slower vs. 68% slower — worse, not better, as the tree got smaller, which ruled out "amortized over more real work" as an explanation and pointed at something structurally fixed-cost). It would later turn out cache behavior is the answer — just not at this size, and not in this direction.

I ran perf stat with hardware counters, first sloppily (both mapTypes in one invocation, contaminating the counts), then correctly split per implementation, then normalized to misses-per-instruction:

Metric (MPKI) BPlusTreeMap JavaTreeMap
L1-dcache MPKI 1.09 3.81
LLC-load-miss MPKI 0.039 0.050
IPC 2.42 2.34

Java's L1 cache-miss rate was 3.5x higher than mine. My IPC was slightly better. Every cache metric available pointed the opposite direction from the theory. The red-black tree's pointer-chasing was generating more cache pressure per instruction, not less. Theory 4 wasn't just unconfirmed — it was actively backwards.

The small-N answer: 2.38x the instructions, uniformly

JMH's -prof perfnorm gave the number that ended the search, reported per-operation instead of per-run, at size=1000:

Metric (per op) BPlusTreeMap JavaTreeMap Ratio
instructions 79.836 33.492 2.38x
cycles 31.032 16.223 1.91x
branches 16.669 8.150 2.05x
branch-misses 0.437 0.190 2.30x
L1-dcache-loads 20.289 9.182 2.21x
LLC-load-misses ≈ 10&strnsup;-4; ≈ 10&strnsup;-3; Java worse

No cache story at this size. No allocation story. No binary-search-scaffolding story. Just: for every single insert, my code executes roughly 2.38x as many instructions as TreeMap does, with every sub-metric (branches, loads, cycles) scaling almost exactly proportionally. My code isn't executing its instructions less efficiently — IPC was actually better than Java's (2.57 vs 2.06) — it's executing more of them.

The two live suspects, both visible in every profile from the first one to the last and never fixed as of this writing:

  1. Collections$ReverseComparator double-dispatch. Every comparison goes through a wrapping comparator object before reaching the real Integer.compare — two virtual calls where a direct comparison needs one. This showed up at 19–21% of self-time in every small-N profile.
  2. merge()'s own per-ascent-level overhead, which grew in relative share from 13.86% at size 10,000 to 23.26% at size 1,000 — a signature of fixed per-call cost that doesn't scale down with tree height, while everything else (search, comparator, allocation) stayed flat or shrank across the same comparison.

Part 2: Why BPlusTreeMap wins at large N (100k–1M) — and why the margin widens

Here's the twist that made a second investigation necessary: the 2.38x instruction gap does not persist. Re-running perfnorm at 100k and 1M:

Metric (per op) 100k BPlus 100k Java 1M BPlus 1M Java
instructions 10,434.483 4,998.309 115,075.033 112,324.769
IPC 1.36 0.58 0.87 0.46
LLC-load-misses 0.385 0.977 223.901 459.609

By 1M, instruction counts have essentially converged (115,075 vs 112,325 — a 2% difference). The "I execute more instructions" story from Part 1 is gone. What's left is IPC collapse: Java's IPC craters to 0.46–0.58 while mine stays at 0.87–1.36. Falling IPC with flat instruction count and rising cycle count is the signature of a CPU stalling on memory, and LLC-load-misses confirm it directly — Java suffers roughly 2–2.5x my miss rate at both sizes.

This is the cache-locality theory from Part 1 — just true at the opposite size and for the opposite reason. At 1k/10k the whole tree fits in cache regardless of shape, so pointer-chasing costs nothing extra. Past 100k, working sets exceed cache capacity, and now every pointer hop in a TreeMap descent (height ≈ log&strnsup;2;(n) ≈ 20 at 1M) is a chance to miss, while my wider fanout (degree 64, height ≈ log&strnsup;64;(n) ≈ 4) needs far fewer hops, each into a cache-friendly contiguous array.

But that still doesn't explain why the margin widens

Cache-miss ratio and IPC ratio between the two implementations stay roughly flat — if anything they move slightly toward parity — between 100k and 1M. So does the allocation-rate ratio: JMH's -prof gc gave exact bytes/op, and Java allocates almost exactly 3.2x more than I do at both sizes (400.036 vs 124.442 B/op at 100k; 4000.696 vs 1250.882 B/op at 1M). A flat 3.2x doesn't explain a widening ns/op gap either. Two solid, real disadvantages for Java — worse cache behavior, more garbage — and neither one's ratio grows with N. Something else had to be compounding.

The real driver: G1's remembered-set cost compounding

The answer came from reading raw GC logs (-Xlog:gc*) line by line instead of trusting aggregate gc.time totals. Every collection in every log — both implementations, both sizes — was a plain young collection; there wasn't a single old-gen or mixed collection anywhere. So the cost isn't about doing old-gen collections. It's about how the young collections behave as old-gen accumulates around them:

GC # JavaTreeMap @ 1M: old regions Evacuate Collection Set
0 1→4 4.85 ms
2 7→10 6.32 ms
4 14→17 7.63 ms
6 22→31 12.63 ms
7 31→44 16.78 ms
GC # BPlusTreeMap @ 1M: old regions Evacuate Collection Set
0 1→4 4.45 ms
2 7→10 6.08 ms
3 10→15 4.51 ms
5 17→18 2.39 ms

Java's per-collection evacuation cost nearly quadruples as old regions climb from 4 to 44 over the course of a single run. Mine falls by almost half over the same kind of run, and old-region growth itself slows to a crawl. Every collection sampled here is young-only — this is G1's remembered set (RSet) at work: every young collection has to scan old-gen→young-gen cross-references to find live roots, and that scan cost grows with how many such cross-references exist.

A red-black tree insert mutates an already-promoted parent's child pointer to point at a brand-new, young-gen node — a fresh old-to-young reference on every single insert, tracked via the write barrier. The more nodes get promoted over the tree's lifetime, the more of these references accumulate, and the more expensive every subsequent young GC becomes to scan — a genuinely compounding cost. My B+Tree mutates a key/child slot inside an existing array, and because the fanout is wide, there are simply far fewer distinct node objects promoted for the same key count — fewer old-to-young references for G1 to track, and RSet-scan cost stays flat or shrinks instead of compounding.

That's the widening-margin mechanism: not allocation rate (flat ratio), not raw cache-miss rate alone (flat-to-shrinking ratio), but GC remembered-set scan cost compounding specifically as a function of total promoted-object count — which is structurally much higher for a node-per-key pointer structure than for a wide-fanout array-node structure, and gets worse, not better, the longer the tree lives and grows.

What actually mattered, in order

  1. Trust the profiler over the architecture diagram — but check both directions. "RBT has no array, so it must be cheaper" was wrong at every size I initially tested. "Array-based B-trees are cache-friendly" was also wrong — at small N. Both statements turned out true, just each one only in the size regime I hadn't looked at yet.
  2. A relabeled cost is not a fixed cost. Rewriting Arrays.binarySearch by hand moved a line in the profile output without moving the benchmark number, because the thing being measured isn't the same as the thing that costs time.
  3. Relative percentages from two different processes are not comparable. 92% of a small allocation total looked worse than 62% of a large one until the absolute bytes were checked.
  4. Normalize before you compare — always. Raw perf stat counts, raw allocation totals, and raw gc.time sums all scale with how long the process ran and how many operations it completed. misses/instruction, bytes/op, and instructions/op were the only numbers that meant anything.
  5. A flat ratio explains a gap; only a widening ratio explains a widening gap. The one metric that actually widened in step with the ns/op gap was per-collection GC pause cost, and only reading raw GC logs line-by-line surfaced it.
  6. The small-N loss and the large-N win are unrelated. Below the point where the tree exceeds cache, this is an instruction-count problem. Above that point, instruction counts converge and it becomes a memory-subsystem problem (cache misses, compounding GC RSet cost).
  7. Cold and warm are not the same benchmark. The raw evidence tables show the cold-start loss itself shrinking with N — five of seven APIs already flip to a BPlusTreeMap win, cold, by 100k. Any crossover story built only on the size axis without separating cold vs. warm risks conflating two different effects.
  8. Check a suspicious number against its siblings before trusting it. A hardcoded @OperationsPerInvocation(100000) silently divided computeIfPresent's reported time by the wrong constant at every size except exactly 100,000 — invisible until it was lined up next to put, merge, and the other single-traversal APIs at the same params and didn't match their scale. The same check later caught a second, still-open 10x discrepancy across an entire 100k rerun. Trust the shape of the whole table, not any one row in isolation.
